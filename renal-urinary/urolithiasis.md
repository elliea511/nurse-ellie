# Urolithiasis

<p class="mh-subtitle">ATI-focused review of kidney, ureter, and bladder stones, stone types, pain patterns, risk factors, diagnostics, complications, diet, medications, lithotripsy, ureteroscopy, and percutaneous removal</p>

**Urolithiasis** means stones anywhere in the urinary tract. Stones may remain in the renal pelvis, move into the ureter, or lodge in the bladder. Nursing priorities are **pain relief, urine flow, hydration, infection prevention, straining urine, stone analysis, and obstruction monitoring**.

### Stone Basics & Types

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Stone Type | Main Cause or Association | Urine Pattern or Key Cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Calcium oxalate** | Most common; hypercalcemia, hyperparathyroidism, sodium, calcium and vitamin D supplements, high oxalate, fat malabsorption | Often acidic urine |
| **Uric acid** | Gout, dehydration, type 2 diabetes, high purine intake, animal protein | Acidic or low-pH urine |
| **Cystine** | Rare inherited amino-acid disorder | Genetic |
| **Struvite** | Chronic UTI with magnesium ammonium phosphate | Alkaline urine |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Calcium Oxalate</strong>

- Most common type
- Hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria increase risk
- Hyperparathyroidism releases calcium from bone
- High sodium leaves more calcium in urine
- Fat malabsorption increases oxalate absorption
- High-oxalate foods contribute
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Uric Acid</strong>

- Too much uric acid crystallizes.
- Gout increases risk.
- Dehydration concentrates urine.
- High-purine animal protein increases uric acid.
- Allopurinol may be prescribed.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Struvite & Cystine</strong>

- Struvite develops with chronic UTI and alkaline urine.
- Cystine stones are rare and genetic.
- Treat the underlying cause.
- Long-term prevention depends on stone analysis.
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-callout" markdown="1">
<strong>Four Stones:</strong> Calcium is common, uric acid goes with gout, cystine is genetic, and struvite goes with infection.
</div>
</div>

### Pain & Manifestations

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Pain Type | Stone Location | Pain Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal colic** | Renal pelvis | Dull, deep aching flank or costovertebral pain |
| **Ureteral colic** | Moving through ureter | Intense, sharp, wave-like pain radiating to the scrotum or vaginal area |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Urinary Findings</strong>

- Hematuria
- Urgency
- Frequent small voids
- Dysuria
- Cloudy urine if infected
- Reduced output if obstructed
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>GI Findings</strong>

- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Restlessness
- Inability to find a comfortable position
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Infection Findings</strong>

- Fever
- Chills
- Cloudy urine
- Dysuria
- Malaise
- Worsening flank pain
</div>
</div>

<div class="nclex-tip" markdown="1">
Pain location helps locate the stone. Deep flank pain suggests the renal pelvis, while sharp wave-like pain radiating to the groin suggests ureteral movement.
</div>
</div>

### Risks, Diagnostics & Complications

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>CRYSTAL Risks</strong>

- **C**onsuming oxalates, purines, salt, or excess supplements
- **R**ecurrent UTIs
- h**Y**percalcemia, hypercalciuria, or hyperparathyroidism
- **S**tructural blockage or urinary stasis
- **T**oo much uric acid
- **A**bsorption problems from GI disease
- **L**ow activity and immobility
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Diagnostics</strong>

- KUB
- IVP
- Ultrasound
- CT scan
- Urinalysis for crystals
- 24-hour urine on ice
- Stone analysis
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Complications</strong>

- Obstruction
- Hydronephrosis
- Hydroureter
- Nephron damage
- Infection
- Reduced renal function
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-callout" markdown="1">
<strong>Obstruction Process:</strong> Stone blocks urine flow → urine backs up → hydrostatic pressure rises → renal pelvis and ureter dilate → nephron filtration falls → prolonged obstruction damages the kidney.
</div>

<div class="report-now" markdown="1">
<h3>Report Immediately</h3>

- Fever with a known stone
- Reduced or absent urine output
- Severe uncontrolled pain
- Hypotension or confusion
- Persistent vomiting
- Signs of sepsis
</div>
</div>

### Nursing Management

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Pain Relief</strong>

- Administer prescribed analgesics.
- NSAIDs may reduce inflammation and help passage.
- Reassess pain.
- Position for comfort.
- Monitor for increasing obstruction.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Fluids</strong>

- Course notes emphasize **3–4 L/day** when not contraindicated.
- Dilutes urine.
- Helps push fragments through.
- Reduces infection risk.
- Monitor intake and output.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Strain All Urine</strong>

- Use a urine strainer every time.
- Save stones and fragments.
- Send for laboratory analysis.
- Stone composition guides prevention.
- Document passage.
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Nursing Action | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Encourage mobility | Helps stone or fragments move |
| Turn frequently | Reduces urinary stasis |
| Monitor I&O | Detects obstruction and hydronephrosis |
| Monitor temperature | Detects infection |
| Assess urine | Identifies blood, cloudiness, and stone fragments |
| Control nausea | Supports hydration |
| Maintain prescribed fluids | Dilutes urine and promotes passage |

</div>
</div>

### Diet & Prevention

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Limit High-Purine Foods</strong>

- Meat
- Fowl
- Fish
- Shellfish
- Lentils
- Dried peas and beans
- Nuts
- Oats
- Beer
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Limit High-Oxalate Foods</strong>

- Spinach
- Rhubarb
- Beets
- Chocolate
- Strawberries
- Cabbage
- Tomatoes
- Nuts
- Wheat bran
- Tea
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Calcium & Sodium Teaching</strong>

- Do not routinely eliminate dietary calcium.
- Restrict calcium only for a specific metabolic reason.
- Avoid excessive calcium and vitamin D supplements.
- Limit sodium to about **2–3 g/day** in the course notes.
- High sodium increases urinary calcium.
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Stone Type | Prevention or Medication |
|---|---|
| **Uric acid** | Allopurinol, fluids, and lower-purine intake |
| **Calcium** | Hydrochlorothiazide may decrease urinary calcium |
| **Struvite** | Prevent and treat urinary infection |
| **All stones** | Maintain hydration and follow stone-analysis diet |

</div>

<div class="nclex-tip" markdown="1">
Limiting all dietary calcium is not routinely recommended. The course emphasizes watching excessive calcium and vitamin D supplements and reducing sodium and oxalates.
</div>
</div>

### Stone Procedures

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Procedure | What Happens | Priority Aftercare |
|---|---|---|
| **ESWL** | External shock waves break a stone into fragments | Fluids 3–4 L/day, strain urine, mobility, pain control, and monitor hematuria |
| **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy** | Incision in the back allows a nephroscope to remove the stone | Monitor nephrostomy tube, infection, drainage, and stone fragments |
| **Ureteroscopy** | Scope enters through urethra to remove or break stones | Stent may remain, strain urine, monitor pain and infection |
| **Stent placement** | Keeps urine flowing and allows fragments to pass | Expect urinary discomfort and monitor patency |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>ESWL</strong>

- Noninvasive
- Not effective for every large stone
- Multiple treatments may be needed
- Fragments must still pass
- Hematuria may occur
- Continue fluids and straining
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Percutaneous Removal</strong>

- Invasive
- Stone removed through the back
- Client does not pass the entire stone naturally
- Nephrostomy tube may be present
- Keep tube secure and patent
- Monitor infection
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Ureteroscopy</strong>

- No external incision
- Scope advances through urinary tract
- Laser or lithotripsy may break stone
- Stent may be placed
- Monitor urine and pain
- Follow stent instructions
</div>
</div>
</div>

<h3>Urolithiasis Big Picture</h3>

| Concept | Main Point | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal colic** | Dull deep flank pain | Assess stone in renal pelvis |
| **Ureteral colic** | Sharp wave-like groin-radiating pain | Control pain and monitor passage |
| **Obstruction** | Urine cannot drain | Protect kidney function |
| **Hydronephrosis** | Urine backs up and dilates kidney | Restore urine flow |
| **Fluids** | 3–4 L/day in course notes | Promote passage and prevent infection |
| **Strain urine** | Captures stone | Send for analysis |
| **ESWL** | Shock waves fragment stone | Expect passage of fragments and possible hematuria |

<div class="mh-columns" markdown="1">
<div class="quick-review" markdown="1">
<h3>Quick Review</h3>

1. Calcium oxalate is the most common stone.
2. Uric-acid stones are linked to gout, dehydration, and purines.
3. Cystine stones are rare and genetic.
4. Struvite stones are linked to chronic UTI and alkaline urine.
5. Renal colic is dull deep flank pain.
6. Ureteral colic is sharp wave-like pain radiating to the groin.
7. Obstruction can cause hydronephrosis and nephron damage.
8. Encourage 3–4 L/day in the course notes when not contraindicated.
9. Strain all urine.
10. Do not routinely eliminate dietary calcium.
11. Limit sodium, oxalates, purines, and excess supplements based on stone type.
12. ESWL breaks stones, but the client must still pass the fragments.
</div>
<div class="do-not-miss" markdown="1">
<h3>Do Not Miss</h3>

- Do not ignore fever with a stone.
- Do not ignore reduced urine output.
- Do not forget to strain every void.
- Do not discard a passed stone.
- Do not restrict all dietary calcium automatically.
- Do not overlook high sodium and oxalate intake.
- Do not forget hematuria can occur after ESWL.
- Do not clamp or kink a nephrostomy tube.
</div>
</div>
