# Kidney Failure & Dialysis

<p class="mh-subtitle">ATI-focused review of kidney failure physiology, acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, fluid and electrolyte complications, anemia, diet, hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, access care, and nursing priorities</p>

Kidney failure prevents removal of hydrogen ions, nitrogen wastes, potassium, phosphorus, creatinine, and water. It also decreases erythropoietin production. Priority threats are **hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary edema, uremia, anemia, infection, bleeding, hypotension, and dialysis complications**.

### Kidney Failure Physiology

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Healthy Kidney Function | Failure Finding | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Removes hydrogen ions | Metabolic acidosis | Low pH and respiratory compensation |
| Removes nitrogen waste | Elevated BUN and uremia | Confusion, fatigue, nausea, and neurologic changes |
| Removes potassium | Hyperkalemia | Life-threatening dysrhythmias |
| Removes phosphorus | Hyperphosphatemia | Calcium falls |
| Activates vitamin D and supports calcium | Hypocalcemia | Neuromuscular and bone problems |
| Removes water | Edema and hypertension | Pulmonary edema risk |
| Produces erythropoietin | Anemia | Fatigue, weakness, and reduced oxygen delivery |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Hyperkalemia</strong>

- Kidneys excrete about 80% of daily potassium in the course notes.
- Potassium rises when kidney function fails.
- Dysrhythmias may occur.
- Peaked T waves are a major warning.
- Cardiac monitoring may be required.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Ammonia & Nitrogen Waste</strong>

- Kidneys normally clear ammonia (NH3).
- Retained ammonia raises BUN and serum ammonia.
- Normal BUN is 10-20; serum ammonia is 15-45.
- Ammonia is toxic to the brain and causes confusion.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Calcium & Phosphorus</strong>

- Phosphorus rises.
- Calcium falls.
- Calcium and vitamin D may be prescribed.
- Phosphate binders lower phosphorus.
- Take phosphate binders with meals.
- Aluminum hydroxide gel is one phosphate binder that binds dietary phosphorus.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Anemia</strong>

- Erythropoietin production decreases.
- Dialysis does not replace erythropoietin.
- Epoetin or Epogen may be prescribed.
- Monitor hemoglobin and hematocrit.
- Fatigue and pallor are common.
</div>
</div>

<div class="report-now" markdown="1">
<h3>Priority Threats</h3>

- Hyperkalemia
- Pulmonary edema
- Severe metabolic acidosis
- Uremic confusion or seizure
- Severe hypertension
- Symptomatic anemia
</div>
</div>

### Acute Kidney Injury

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Prerenal Causes</strong>

- Shock
- Severe hypotension
- Hemorrhage
- Dehydration
- Severe illness
- Reduced renal blood flow
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Intrarenal Causes</strong>

- Direct kidney damage
- Inflammation
- Toxins
- Infection
- Reduced blood supply
- Gentamicin nephrotoxicity
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Postrenal Causes</strong>

- Enlarged prostate
- Kidney stones
- Bladder tumors
- Injury
- Sudden urinary obstruction
- Bilateral outflow blockage
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| AKI Phase | Course Findings | Priority Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Oliguric phase** | Urine output below **400 mL/24 hr**, lasts **1–2 weeks** | Fluid overload, hyperkalemia, acidosis, rising wastes |
| **Diuretic phase** | Gradual increase in dilute urine, lasts **2–3 weeks** | Dehydration and electrolyte loss |
| **Recovery phase** | Renal function stabilizes and improves over **3–12 months** | Continue monitoring until function returns |

</div>

<div class="detail-callout" markdown="1">
<strong>AKI Direction:</strong> Acute kidney injury starts with oliguria and improves into polyuria during the diuretic phase.
</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Oliguric Priorities</strong>

- Restrict fluids as prescribed.
- Restrict potassium.
- Monitor daily weight.
- Monitor lungs.
- Monitor cardiac rhythm.
- Track BUN and creatinine.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Diuretic Priorities</strong>

- Measure large urine losses.
- Replace fluids as ordered.
- Monitor blood pressure.
- Monitor sodium and potassium.
- Prevent falls.
- Watch dehydration.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Recovery</strong>

- Kidney function may return.
- Recovery may take months.
- Avoid nephrotoxins.
- Continue laboratory follow-up.
- Teach hydration and medication safety.
</div>
</div>
</div>

### Chronic Kidney Disease

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>What CKD Is</strong>

- Progressive
- Irreversible
- Nephrons are replaced by scar tissue
- Develops gradually
- Diabetes and chronic glomerulonephritis are major course causes
- Ends in renal replacement need
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Early Diminished Reserve</strong>

- Polyuria
- Dilute urine
- Kidneys compensate by making more urine
- Chemistry values may remain normal initially
- Client may not recognize progression
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Advanced Failure</strong>

- Oliguria
- Uremia
- Edema
- Hyperkalemia
- Acidosis
- Anemia
- Pruritus
- Neurologic changes
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-callout" markdown="1">
<strong>AKI vs. CKD Direction:</strong> AKI starts oliguric and improves to polyuria. CKD starts with compensatory polyuria and progresses to oliguria.
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| CKD Finding | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Uremic frost** | Waste crystals appear on skin because the body cannot excrete them in urine |
| **Severe itching** | Uremic deposits irritate the skin |
| **Anemia** | Reduced erythropoietin |
| **Edema** | Water and sodium retention |
| **Confusion** | Uremic toxins and electrolyte imbalance |
| **Bone problems** | High phosphorus, low calcium, and low active vitamin D |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Uremic Frost Care</strong>

- Gently wash waste crystals from skin.
- Apply prescribed anti-itch lotion.
- Avoid scratching.
- Keep nails short.
- Assess for skin breakdown.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Final D Options</strong>

- Dialysis
- Donor transplant
- Death without renal replacement
- Course memory cue: **Dialysis, Donor, or Death**
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Irreversibility</strong>

- CKD damage does not recover.
- Treatment slows progression.
- Complications require management.
- Dialysis or transplant replaces lost function.
</div>
</div>
</div>

### Nursing Management & Diet

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Core Monitoring</strong>

- Strict intake and output
- Daily weights
- Blood pressure
- Lung sounds
- Edema
- Cardiac rhythm
- Mental status
- Laboratory trends
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Diet</strong>

- Low protein
- High carbohydrate
- Low sodium
- Low potassium when elevated or oliguric
- Low phosphorus
- Fluid restriction when prescribed
- Adequate calories
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Skin & Safety</strong>

- Gentle bathing
- Remove uremic frost
- Anti-itch lotion
- Repositioning
- Fall precautions
- Oral care
- Avoid injury and bleeding
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Medication or Treatment | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Epoetin or Epogen** | Replaces erythropoietin effect and treats anemia |
| **Calcium and vitamin D** | Treat low calcium and bone problems |
| **Phosphate binders** (e.g., aluminum hydroxide gel) | Bind phosphorus in the GI tract and are taken with meals |
| **Antihypertensives** | Control blood pressure |
| **Potassium-lowering therapy** | Treat dangerous hyperkalemia |
| **Dialysis** | Removes wastes, corrects acidosis, balances electrolytes, and removes fluid |

</div>

<div class="nclex-tip" markdown="1">
Daily weight is the best routine indicator of fluid change. One kilogram of weight change is approximately one liter of fluid.
</div>
</div>

### Hemodialysis

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>What Hemodialysis Does</strong>

- Removes protein-metabolism wastes
- Maintains safer electrolytes
- Corrects acidosis
- Removes excess fluid
- Does not replace erythropoietin
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>AV Access Assessment</strong>

- Palpate for thrill.
- Auscultate for bruit.
- Access should feel warm.
- Cool access or dark areas may suggest clotting.
- Protect from pressure.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Protect the Access Arm</strong>

- No blood pressure
- No venipuncture
- No IV medication
- No constrictive clothing
- No heavy lifting
- Do not sleep on the arm
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Fistula Teaching</strong>

- Matures about 6 weeks before first use.
- Squeeze a rubber ball to strengthen the vein.
- Report numbness, tingling, or a pale, cool hand.
- Watch for infection at the site.
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Hemodialysis Timing | Nursing Care |
|---|---|
| **Before** | Ask client to void, obtain weight, assess vital signs, assess access, review labs |
| **Medication timing** | Withhold vasodilators and water-soluble drugs when ordered |
| **During** | Monitor BP, access, connections, bleeding, cramps, nausea, and neurologic status |
| **After** | Obtain weight, reassess vital signs, inspect access, monitor hypotension and bleeding |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Why BP Drops</strong>

- Fluid is removed rapidly.
- Vasodilators worsen hypotension.
- The client may become dizzy.
- Use fall precautions.
- Reassess after dialysis.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Why Bleeding Occurs</strong>

- Blood is heparinized in the machine.
- Connections can loosen.
- Access puncture sites can bleed.
- Monitor closely.
- Apply pressure as ordered.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Disequilibrium Syndrome</strong>

Dialysis removes wastes from the blood faster than they leave the brain. Water then shifts into the brain cells and causes cerebral swelling, usually during or right after treatment. Watch for:

- Headache
- Nausea
- Restlessness
- Confusion
- Seizure
</div>
</div>

<div class="report-now" markdown="1">
<h3>Report Immediately</h3>

- No thrill or bruit
- Cool or dark access
- Uncontrolled bleeding
- Severe hypotension
- Chest pain
- New confusion or seizure
- Respiratory distress
</div>
</div>

### Peritoneal Dialysis

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>How It Works</strong>

- Dialysate enters the abdomen.
- Peritoneal membrane acts as the filter.
- Wastes and fluid move into dialysate.
- Fluid dwells for a prescribed time.
- Dialysate drains by gravity.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Cycle</strong>

- Infuse
- Dwell
- Drain
- Repeat
- Maintain sterile technique
- Warm dialysate as directed
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Diet Difference</strong>

- More protein may be allowed.
- Protein is lost in dialysate.
- Monitor nutritional status.
- Monitor glucose and weight.
- Follow individualized diet.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Types & Uses</strong>

- Treats both acute and chronic kidney failure.
- Acute: many rapid exchanges with short dwells.
- CAPD (chronic): manual; client stays ambulatory during the dwell.
- APD (chronic): a machine cycles exchanges overnight.
- Chronic dwells run longer (about 4-6 hours).
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-table" markdown="1">

| Effluent or Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Clear pale-yellow effluent** | Expected |
| **Cloudy dialysate** | Infection or peritonitis |
| **Abdominal pain and fever** | Peritonitis concern |
| **Poor outflow** | Check position, kinks, constipation, and obstruction |
| **Respiratory difficulty** | Dialysate may be limiting diaphragm movement |
| **Hyperglycemia or weight gain** | Glucose absorption from dialysate |

</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Prevent Infection</strong>

- Sterile technique
- Mask when system is open if required
- Careful connection handling
- Clean exit site
- Monitor temperature
- Inspect drainage
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Improve Outflow</strong>

- Reposition the client.
- Check clamps.
- Check tubing for kinks.
- Assess constipation.
- Keep drainage bag below abdomen.
- Do not force fluid.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Major Complications</strong>

- Peritonitis
- Protein loss
- Hyperglycemia
- Weight gain
- Hernia
- Respiratory compromise
- Poor drainage
</div>
</div>

<div class="report-now" markdown="1">
<h3>Cloudy Dialysate = Report</h3>

Cloudy peritoneal effluent is the classic cue for peritonitis and requires immediate follow-up.
</div>
</div>

### Kidney Transplant

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Donor Selection</strong>

- Donor and recipient must be tissue compatible (antigen match).
- More matched antigens mean a better chance of success.
- Best match is an identical twin, then a sibling.
- Next best is a parent or child (about half the gene pool).
- Living donors can give a kidney or part of the liver.
- Cadaver donors are often previously healthy trauma victims who are now brain dead.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Organ-Donor Teaching</strong>

- Encourage carrying an organ-donor card and noting it on the driver's license.
- Encourage discussing wishes with family ahead of time.
- The next of kin makes the decision, so clear wishes ease that choice.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Immunosuppressants (For Life)</strong>

- Cyclosporine (Sandimmune)
- Azathioprine (Imuran)
- Antilymphocytic globulin (ALG)
- Corticosteroids
- Usually a steroid plus one or more of the others.
- Teach the client about living with long-term immunosuppression and infection risk.
</div>
</div>

<div class="detail-callout" markdown="1">
<strong>Placement:</strong> The transplanted kidney is usually placed in the iliac (hip) fossa, with a ureter and blood supply attached. It often begins working before surgery ends, so a client may have three kidneys total.
</div>

<div class="detail-grid" markdown="1">
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Routine Postoperative Care</strong>

- Monitor fluid and electrolyte balance with hourly I&O.
- Expect possible massive diuresis early.
- Give immunosuppressive agents for life.
- Assess continuously for signs of acute rejection.
</div>
<div class="detail-mini-card" markdown="1"><strong>Acute Rejection</strong>

- Occurs 4 days to 4 months after transplant.
- Decreased urine output.
- Fever.
- Pain or tenderness over the transplant site.
- Edema and weight gain.
- Rising serum creatinine.
</div>
</div>

<div class="nclex-tip" markdown="1">
Acute rejection shows signs of organ failure plus pain and fever. A rising creatinine with decreased urine output over the graft is a key warning.
</div>
</div>

<div class="mh-table-card" markdown="1">

<h3>Kidney Failure & Dialysis Big Picture</h3>

| Concept | Main Point | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperkalemia** | Potassium cannot be excreted | Cardiac monitoring and rapid treatment |
| **AKI** | Abrupt and potentially reversible | Identify cause and support phases |
| **CKD** | Progressive and irreversible | Control complications and plan replacement |
| **Early CKD** | Polyuria and dilute urine | Compensation |
| **Late CKD** | Oliguria and uremia | Dialysis or transplant |
| **Hemodialysis access** | Lifeline | Thrill, bruit, and arm protection |
| **Peritoneal dialysis** | Peritoneum filters blood | Sterility and clear effluent |
| **Daily weight** | Fluid indicator | Trend every day |

</div>

<div class="quick-review" markdown="1">
<h3>Quick Review</h3>

1. Kidney failure causes acidosis, hyperkalemia, high phosphorus, low calcium, edema, and anemia.
2. Hyperkalemia is a major cardiac emergency.
3. Dialysis does not replace erythropoietin.
4. AKI has oliguric, diuretic, and recovery phases.
5. AKI begins with oliguria and improves to polyuria.
6. CKD begins with compensatory polyuria and progresses to oliguria.
7. CKD is irreversible.
8. Daily weight is the best routine fluid indicator.
9. Assess an AV access for a thrill and bruit.
10. Never use the access arm for BP or venipuncture.
11. Hemodialysis can cause hypotension and bleeding.
12. Clear pale-yellow peritoneal effluent is expected.
13. Cloudy effluent suggests peritonitis.
14. Peritoneal dialysis clients may need more protein.
</div>
<div class="do-not-miss" markdown="1">
<h3>Do Not Miss</h3>

- Do not ignore peaked T waves.
- Do not confuse the AKI and CKD urine patterns.
- Do not overlook fluid overload and crackles.
- Do not use a dialysis-access arm for procedures.
- Do not ignore loss of thrill or bruit.
- Do not give a vasodilator before dialysis when it is ordered held.
- Do not ignore neurologic changes after dialysis.
- Do not ignore cloudy peritoneal effluent.
</div>
</div>
